The Songbook

The real songs behind Belenon's Saga creatures: what each one is about, a little music history, and how every chapter on the card plays its part.

Every song tells a story. Magic's Sagas tell theirs a chapter at a time: a lore counter each turn, a new chapter, and the tale moves on. The best Sagas follow their story's shape, with setup, conflict and aftermath, and mechanics that match what happens (a reanimation for a return from the dead, a sweeper that grows as an eruption builds). Some repeat a chapter when the story repeats, and a few even take their chapter count from the source.

On Belenon, the songs are real. The Seam, a broken Omenpath torn loose by the returning Winds, points past the Multiverse into the storied worlds, and it opens to music. When Belenon's musicians play a famous song to its final bar, the song's own performer steps through. So every Saga creature here is two things: the song, performed by Belenon's guilds, and on its back the legend who wrote or made it famous, called through by the ending.

How to read an entry: the story of the song, a few liner notes from music history, and then the card chapter by chapter: what each one does, and which part of the song it plays.

Cadenza Conservatory · Classical

Common · Ballet

Swan Lake

Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky · 1877

Prince Siegfried, told he must choose a bride, wanders to a moonlit lake and finds Odette, a princess cursed by the sorcerer Rothbart to live as a swan by day. Only a vow of true love can break the spell. At the palace ball, Rothbart brings his daughter Odile, disguised as Odette, and Siegfried pledges himself to the wrong swan. The ballet ends at the lake, in grief, with the lovers lost to the water.

Liner notes

Tchaikovsky's first ballet premiered at Moscow's Bolshoi Theatre in 1877 and made little impression: the staging was weak, and some critics found the score too symphonic for ballet. It only became the ballet of ballets in 1895, in Marius Petipa and Lev Ivanov's revival in St. Petersburg, two years after the composer's death. Its white-and-black double role, Odette and Odile danced by one ballerina, is still one of the hardest parts in dance.

Uncommon · Chamber music

Piano Trio in G Minor

Clara Schumann · 1846

A trio for piano, violin and cello in four movements: a stormy Allegro moderato, a playful Scherzo in the time of a minuet, a slow and singing Andante, and an Allegretto finale that weaves a fugue into its drive. It has no plot, only a conversation between three instruments that argue, dance, console each other and finish together.

Liner notes

Clara Wieck was a concert star at eleven and one of the great pianists of the nineteenth century, touring Europe for six decades. She married the composer Robert Schumann over her father's furious objection and raised eight children while performing and composing. Her Piano Trio, written in 1846, is often called her masterpiece. She doubted herself anyway, writing in her diary that a woman must not desire to compose. Her portrait was on Germany's 100-mark note.

Rare · Keyboard music

The Well-Tempered Clavier

Johann Sebastian Bach · 1722 and 1742

Not one piece but forty-eight: two books, each with a prelude and fugue in every one of the twenty-four major and minor keys. Each prelude is a free-flowing warm-up; each fugue takes a single short melody, the subject, and passes it from voice to voice, overlapping itself, until the whole keyboard is singing it at once.

Liner notes

Bach wrote the first book in 1722 for "the profit and use of musical youth desirous of learning," and the second about twenty years later. Its title points to a tuning system that let a keyboard play in every key, still a new idea then. It became the textbook of Western harmony: Mozart arranged its fugues, Beethoven played it as a boy, and Chopin warmed up with it before concerts. The first Prelude in C major, a ripple of broken chords, is one of the most played pieces ever written.

    The prelude, which prepares its fugue: look ahead, then take a card.

  2. II

    Return up to one target Saga card from your graveyard to your hand.

    The fugue: the subject comes back in voice after voice. A Saga returns to your hand to be played again.

  3. III

    Exile this Saga, then return it to the battlefield transformed under your control.

    Bach himself, who keeps the counterpoint going: each Saga you cast moves another one forward.

If I Ruled the World

Nas, with Lauryn Hill · 1996

Nas imagines a different world, one where the people from his neighborhood walk free, the poor share in the wealth, and nobody is held back by where they were born. Lauryn Hill sings the hook, and the whole song stays in the conditional: this is what he would do if he ran things.

Liner notes

Nas grew up in Queensbridge, the largest public housing project in North America, and his 1994 debut Illmatic is regularly named the greatest hip-hop album ever made. "If I Ruled the World (Imagine That)," from his second album It Was Written, was his biggest hit yet, built on Kurtis Blow's 1985 song of the same name, and it brought him to radio while Lauryn Hill was at the height of the Fugees.

An order to the dance floor: let go, move, and don't stop. Missy boasts, taunts her rivals and rides a beat built from a twanging Punjabi string riff and tabla drums, opening with a line in Japanese.

Missy Elliott and her producer Timbaland grew up together in Virginia and spent the late 1990s making records that sounded like nothing else on the radio. Rare · Hip-hop

Hypnotize

The Notorious B.I.G. · 1997

Biggie's victory lap: the money, the cars, the parties and the way he holds a room, delivered so smoothly that the listener is under his spell. The chorus, sung by Pamela Long, says it outright: he has a way of hypnotizing.

Liner notes

Christopher Wallace grew up in Brooklyn and became the voice of East Coast rap with Ready to Die in 1994. "Hypnotize" was the lead single from Life After Death. He was killed in Los Angeles in March 1997, days after its release, and it became his first No. 1 after his death. The beat, produced by Puff Daddy's team, is built on Herb Alpert's 1979 instrumental "Rise," and the hook borrows from Slick Rick's "La Di Da Di": sampling as tribute, the way rap has always honored its elders.

The most famous diss track ever recorded: Tupac, with the Outlawz behind him, turning on his former friend the Notorious B.I.G. and the whole Bad Boy camp, holding nothing back. A song written to take a rival apart in public.

Tupac recorded constantly, and more of his albums came out after his death than during his life.

Ozzy Osbourne · 1980

Written in the shadow of the Cold War, it's about a world gone mad: people raised to fear each other, a planet arming itself to the teeth, and everyone riding a train that's heading off the rails. The madness is the point, and the riff is the train.

Rhoads died in a plane crash in 1982. The bassist Bob Daisley wrote most of the words.

Master of Puppets

Metallica · 1986

Addiction told from the drug's point of view: the master pulling the strings, the victim dancing to its commands, begging and obeying. More than eight minutes of thrash break into a quiet, melodic interlude in the middle, like a moment of clarity, before the master drags it back.

The band's nickname on the card comes from "The Four Horsemen," a song on their 1983 debut.

Rare · Rock

Bohemian Rhapsody

Queen · 1975

A young man confesses to his mother that he has killed a man and must face what's coming. His fate is argued over in a mock opera of voices, he rages against it in a burst of hard rock, and in the end he decides that nothing really matters, as the wind blows on.

Liner notes

Freddie Mercury wrote it as several songs joined into one: an a cappella intro, a ballad, a guitar solo, an operatic section and a hard-rock section, then an outro, with no chorus at all. The record label thought six minutes was too long for radio; a DJ played it anyway, and it spent nine weeks at No. 1 in Britain. It topped the charts again in 1991 after Mercury's death, and climbed to No. 2 in the US when Wayne's World put it on screen in 1992.

Rare · Bebop

Ornithology

Charlie Parker · 1946

No words, only flight. Bebop tunes open with the head, the melody played by the horns together, then the players take turns soloing over the chords before the head returns. "Ornithology" sets a new, darting melody over the chords of the old standard "How High the Moon."

Liner notes

Charlie Parker, nicknamed Yardbird or just Bird, helped invent bebop in 1940s New York: fast, harmonically daring jazz for listening instead of dancing. Writing new melodies over an existing song's chords, a contrafact, was a favorite bebop trick. "Ornithology," written with the trumpeter Benny Harris, has a bird joke for a title and was recorded in 1946 with a 19-year-old Miles Davis on trumpet. New York's famous jazz club Birdland was named after Parker.

Requiem in D Minor

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart · 1791

A mass for the dead in many parts: the Introitus, praying for eternal rest; the Dies irae, the day of wrath; and the Lacrimosa, the day of tears. Mozart never finished it.

Liner notes

In 1791 a stranger brought Mozart an anonymous commission for a requiem. It came from Count Walsegg, who meant to pass it off as his own work in memory of his late wife. Mozart fell ill while writing it and died that December, at 35, eight bars into the Lacrimosa. His student Franz Xaver Süssmayr finished it. The legend that grew up, that Mozart believed he was writing his own requiem, inspired Peter Shaffer's play and the film Amadeus, which made Antonio Salieri his rival.
